Engineering · Shared Core, Year 2

Safety and Industrial Training Preparation

Most injuries on a site follow a small number of causes: noise, fire, electricity, heights, chemicals, heat and lifting. Each has a measurable limit and a standard control. This course also prepares the student for the industrial placement: the logbook, the supervisors, the cost and the conduct. One hundred problems, every step shown.

ORIENTATION

Hazard, control, record

A hazard is found, its risk is scored, and the controls are applied from the most effective down to protective equipment. What remains is the residual risk, which must be low enough to accept. The record of the work, whether an incident report or a placement logbook, is the evidence that the steps were taken.

CHAPTER 1

The Ten Blocks

One card per block, with the rule and the trap to avoid.

Block 1 · Hazard and risk

Score and control

risk = L × S
Rule: Remove the hazard first. PPE is last. A guard lowers likelihood only; severity is unchanged.
Block 2 · Safety statistics

Rates, not counts

LTIFR = N × 10⁶ / h
Rule: Compare rates when hours differ. Small numbers swing widely from year to year.
Block 3 · Noise and hearing

Add energy, not decibels

+3 dB per doubling
Rule: Each 3 dB halves the permitted time. Sum the fractions of time to get the dose.
Block 4 · Fire and explosion

Break the triangle

LEL to UEL
Rule: Never use water on live electrical equipment or oil. Set alarms at a fraction of the LEL.
Block 5 · Electrical safety

Current kills, not voltage

I = V / R
Rule: The RCD watches leakage. The breaker watches overload. Prove the circuit dead before work.
Block 6 · Mechanical hazards

Lifting, heights, guarding

T = W / (2 sin θ)
Rule: Shallow sling angles raise the leg tension. Fall factor is at most 2. Guard distance follows stop time.
Block 7 · Chemical and environmental

Dose and ventilation

ppm × M / 24.45
Rule: Time-weight the exposure. Test confined spaces before entry. Add acid to water.
Block 8 · Ergonomics and climate

Light, heat, posture

N = EA / (F UF MF)
Rule: Hold loads close to the spine. Use the right WBGT formula for sun or shade.
Block 9 · SIWES and the logbook

Hours, records, supervisors

weeks × days × hours
Rule: The industry supervisor signs weekly. Plan the cost of transport and lunch before you start.
Block 10 · Emergency and conduct

First aid and reporting

30 : 2
Rule: Cool burns for 20 minutes. Report missing people. Decline tasks you are not trained for.
CHAPTER 2 · BLOCK 1

Hazard and risk

Questions 1–10. Score risk and choose controls in order.

Question 1
Separate the hazard from the risk: a drum of acid stands in a corridor, and a worker might knock it over and be splashed.
Solution
1A hazard is something with the potential to cause harm: the acid in the drum.
2A risk is the chance that the harm happens, together with how bad it would be: being knocked over and splashed.
3Moving the drum removes the exposure; the acid is still a hazard.
Answerhazard: the acid; risk: the chance and severity of a splash injury
Question 2
A task has likelihood 3 and severity 4 on 1–5 scales. Score the risk. Take 1–4 as low, 5–12 as medium and 15–25 as high.
Solution
1Risk = likelihood × severity = 3 × 4 = 12.
212 falls in the medium band.
Answer12, medium
Common slips: A score on the boundary of a band deserves a second look before it is accepted.
Question 3
Rank these controls for a noisy compressor, best first: ear defenders, an acoustic enclosure, a quieter compressor, rotating workers, removing the compressor.
Solution
1The order is elimination, substitution, engineering controls, administrative controls and PPE.
2Remove the compressor; replace it with a quieter one; enclose it; rotate workers; issue ear defenders.
Answerremove, quieter unit, enclosure, rotation, ear defenders
Question 4
A task scores 5 × 4 = 20. A guard cuts the likelihood to 2. Find the residual risk.
Solution
1Residual risk = 2 × 4 = 8.
2Severity is unchanged: the guard only makes the event less likely.
Answer8
Question 5
Find the percentage reduction in risk in the last problem.
Solution
1Reduction = (20 − 8)/20.
2= 12/20 = 60%.
Answer60%
Question 6
List the five steps of a risk assessment.
Solution
1Identify the hazards.
2Decide who might be harmed and how.
3Evaluate the risks and decide the controls.
4Record the findings and tell the people involved.
5Review the assessment when the work changes.
Answerhazards; who is harmed; evaluate and control; record; review
Question 7
Break the task ‘change a grinding wheel’ into steps and name one hazard for each.
Solution
11. Isolate the grinder: hazard of unexpected start.
22. Remove the guard and old wheel: hazard of trapped fingers and a cracked wheel.
33. Inspect and ring-test the new wheel: hazard of a hidden crack.
44. Fit, tighten the guard and run idle for a minute behind a screen: hazard of a bursting wheel.
Answerisolate, remove, inspect, fit and test, each with its hazard
Question 8
Why is PPE the last choice among controls?
Solution
1PPE leaves the hazard in place.
2It works only if it is chosen correctly, fits, is worn all the time and is kept in good condition.
3Elimination and engineering controls protect everyone without depending on behaviour.
Answerit relies on the worker and does not remove the hazard
Question 9
Classify as unsafe act or unsafe condition: (a) a guard is missing; (b) the operator removed the guard to clear a jam while the machine ran.
Solution
1A condition is the state of the workplace: (a).
2An act is something a person does: (b).
3Both need a cause found: why was the guard missing, why could the guard be removed while running?
Answer(a) unsafe condition; (b) unsafe act
Question 10
Using the 1 : 29 : 300 ratio, estimate the minor injuries and no-injury events behind 3 major injuries.
Solution
1Minor injuries = 3 × 29 = 87.
2No-injury events = 3 × 300 = 900.
3Near-miss reports are the cheapest warning.
Answer87 minor injuries; 900 no-injury events
Common slips: The ratio is a rule of thumb from one study, not a law, but it shows why minor events matter.
CHAPTER 2 · BLOCK 2

Safety statistics

Questions 11–20. Injury rates and what they show.

Question 11
A contractor logs 4 lost-time injuries in 800 000 hours worked. Find the lost-time injury frequency rate per million hours.
Solution
1LTIFR = injuries × 1 000 000/hours.
2= 4 × 106/800 000 = 5.0.
Answer5.0
Question 12
The same injuries cost 60 days in total. Find the severity rate per million hours.
Solution
1Severity rate = days lost × 106/hours.
2= 60 × 106/800 000 = 75.
Answer75
Question 13
A site has 250 workers, each working 2000 hours a year, with 2 lost-time injuries. Find the LTIFR.
Solution
1Hours = 250 × 2000 = 500 000.
2LTIFR = 2 × 106/500 000 = 4.0.
Answer4.0
Question 14
A firm records 6 recordable cases in 400 000 hours. Find the incidence rate per 200 000 hours (100 full-time workers a year).
Solution
1Rate = cases × 200 000/hours.
2= 6 × 200 000/400 000 = 3.0.
Answer3.0
Common slips: The base of 200 000 hours is 100 workers × 40 hours × 50 weeks.
Question 15
A plant logged 150 near misses and 5 injuries in a year. Find the near misses per injury.
Solution
1150/5 = 30.
2A high ratio shows people are reporting; a ratio near zero usually means events are being missed.
Answer30 near misses per injury
Question 16
An injury has direct costs of ₦200 000. Assume the indirect costs are four times the direct costs. Find the total.
Solution
1Indirect = 4 × 200 000 = ₦800 000.
2Total = 200 000 + 800 000 = ₦1 000 000.
Answer₦1 000 000
Common slips: The factor of four is an assumption and varies with the industry.
Question 17
Site A had 3 injuries in 300 000 hours and Site B had 5 in 700 000 hours. Which is safer per hour?
Solution
1Site A: 3 × 106/300 000 = 10.0.
2Site B: 5 × 106/700 000 = 7.14.
3Site B has the lower rate, though it has more injuries.
AnswerSite B: 7.14 against 10.0
Common slips: Compare rates, never raw counts, when hours differ.
Question 18
A company’s LTIFR fell from 8 to 5. Find the percentage reduction.
Solution
1Reduction = (8 − 5)/8.
2= 0.375, or 37.5%.
Answer37.5%
Question 19
A job has a fatal accident rate of 4 per 108 hours. Over a 40-year career of 2000 hours a year, find the chance of death.
Solution
1Hours = 40 × 2000 = 80 000.
2Chance = 4 × 80 000/108 = 0.0032, or 0.32%.
3That is about 1 in 312.
Answer0.32%, about 1 in 312
Question 20
A firm had 1 lost-time injury in 100 000 hours one year (rate 10) and 2 the next (rate 20). Is the firm twice as dangerous?
Solution
1The rate doubled, but only one more injury occurred.
2With such small numbers chance alone can produce this change.
3Look at causes, near misses and longer trends before drawing a conclusion.
Answernot necessarily; the numbers are too small
CHAPTER 2 · BLOCK 3

Noise and hearing

Questions 21–30. Combining levels, distance, permitted time and dose.

Question 21
Two identical machines each give 85 dB at a point. Find the combined level.
Solution
1Doubling the sound energy adds 10 log 2 = 3 dB.
285 + 3 = 88 dB.
Answer88 dB
Common slips: Decibels do not add arithmetically: 85 + 85 is not 170.
Question 22
A 90 dB source and an 84 dB source operate together. Find the combined level.
Solution
1L = 10 log(109.0 + 108.4) = 10 log(1.0 × 109 + 2.51 × 108).
2= 10 log(1.251 × 109) = 91.0 dB.
Answer91.0 dB
Common slips: A source 6 dB quieter adds only 1 dB to the louder one.
Question 23
A small source gives 100 dB at 1 m. Find the level at 8 m in the open.
Solution
1For a point source, level falls by 20 log(d2/d1).
2= 20 log 8 = 18.1 dB.
3Level = 100 − 18.1 = 81.9 dB.
Answer81.9 dB
Question 24
The limit is 85 dBA for 8 h, with a 3 dB exchange rate. Find the permitted time at 91 dBA.
Solution
1T = 8/2(L − 85)/3.
2= 8/22 = 2 h.
Answer2 h
Question 25
A worker spends 4 h at 88 dBA and 2 h at 91 dBA. Find the noise dose.
Solution
1Permitted times: 88 dBA gives 4 h; 91 dBA gives 2 h.
2Dose = 4/4 + 2/2 = 2.0, which is 200%.
3The limit of 100% is exceeded.
Answer200%
Question 26
Express that dose as an 8-hour equivalent level.
Solution
1Level = 85 + 10 log(dose).
2= 85 + 10 log 2 = 88.0 dBA.
Answer88 dBA
Question 27
Ear plugs have a noise reduction rating of 25. Use the derating (NRR − 7)/2 to estimate the protection and the level at the ear in a 98 dBA area.
Solution
1Protection = (25 − 7)/2 = 9 dB.
2Level at the ear = 98 − 9 = 89 dBA.
3That is still above 85 dBA.
Answer9 dB; 89 dBA
Common slips: Laboratory ratings overstate what workers achieve; that is why they are derated.
Question 28
A worker spends 8 h at 82 dBA. Find the dose.
Solution
1Permitted time = 8 × 2(85 − 82)/3 = 8 × 2 = 16 h.
2Dose = 8/16 = 0.5, or 50%.
Answer50%
Question 29
An enclosure cuts a machine’s level by 15 dB. The level at the operator is 98 dBA. Find the new level.
Solution
198 − 15 = 83 dBA.
2It is now below 85 dBA, so protection is no longer needed at that spot.
Answer83 dBA
Question 30
A source gives 100 dBA at 1 m. How far away does the level drop to 85 dBA?
Solution
1Drop needed = 15 dB = 20 log d.
2log d = 0.75, so d = 100.75 = 5.6 m.
3Mark a hearing-protection zone within about 6 m.
Answer5.6 m
CHAPTER 2 · BLOCK 4

Fire and explosion

Questions 31–40. The triangle, extinguishers and explosive limits.

Question 31
The fire triangle has three sides. Name them and give one way to break each.
Solution
1Fuel: remove it, for example by closing a fuel valve or clearing combustible stock.
2Heat: cool it, with water.
3Oxygen: smother it, with foam, a blanket or carbon dioxide.
Answerfuel, heat, oxygen; remove, cool, smother
Common slips: A fourth side, the chain reaction, is broken by dry powder.
Question 32
Choose the extinguisher: (a) cooking oil; (b) energised electrical equipment; (c) paper and wood; (d) petrol.
Solution
1(a) cooking oil, class F: wet chemical, or a fire blanket on a small fire.
2(b) electrical: carbon dioxide, which does not conduct.
3(c) class A solids: water.
4(d) class B liquids: foam or dry powder.
Answer(a) wet chemical or blanket; (b) CO2; (c) water; (d) foam or powder
Question 33
Which of water, carbon dioxide and dry powder must not be used on live electrical equipment?
Solution
1Water conducts electricity and can carry current back to the user.
2Carbon dioxide and dry powder do not conduct.
Answerwater
Question 34
Methane burns in air between 5% and 15% by volume. Classify mixtures of 3%, 8% and 20%.
Solution
13% is below the lower explosive limit: too lean.
28% lies between the limits: explosive.
320% is above the upper limit: too rich, though it will burn where it mixes with more air.
Answer3% too lean; 8% explosive; 20% too rich
Question 35
A gas alarm is set at 20% of the lower explosive limit for methane. Find the setting by volume.
Solution
1Setting = 0.20 × 5% = 1.0% methane in air.
Answer1.0% by volume
Question 36
Petrol has a flash point near −43 °C and diesel above 55 °C. Which gives off ignitable vapour at a 30 °C ambient?
Solution
1A liquid gives ignitable vapour once the temperature is above its flash point.
2Petrol is far above its flash point; diesel is below its flash point.
Answerpetrol
Question 37
An exit is 36 m away. People walk at 1.2 m/s, and recognition and response take 60 s. Find the time to get out.
Solution
1Walking time = 36/1.2 = 30 s.
2Total = 60 + 30 = 90 s.
Answer90 s
Common slips: Delay before moving is usually longer than the walk itself.
Question 38
A floor of 900 m2 needs one extinguisher per 200 m2. How many?
Solution
1900/200 = 4.5.
2Round up: 5 extinguishers, placed so that no point is beyond the code’s travel distance.
Answer5
Question 39
Which detector suits a kitchen: smoke or heat?
Solution
1Cooking produces smoke and steam that set off smoke detectors falsely.
2A heat detector responds to a rise in temperature and suits kitchens.
Answerheat detector
Question 40
State the actions on finding a fire (RACE) and the use of an extinguisher (PASS).
Solution
1RACE: rescue people at risk, raise the alarm, contain by closing doors, then extinguish or evacuate.
2PASS: pull the pin, aim at the base, squeeze the handle, sweep side to side.
3Fight a fire only if it is small and you have a clear exit behind you.
AnswerRACE; PASS
CHAPTER 2 · BLOCK 5

Electrical safety

Questions 41–50. Body current, protective devices and isolation.

Question 41
A person touches 230 V with a body resistance of 1000 Ω between hands. Find the current.
Solution
1I = V/R = 230/1000 = 0.23 A.
2= 230 mA, which is well above the level that can cause fibrillation.
Answer230 mA
Question 42
Classify the effect of 0.5 mA, 15 mA and 100 mA through the body for about a second.
Solution
10.5 mA is below the threshold of perception.
215 mA is above the let-go level of about 10 mA, so the muscles may not release the conductor.
3100 mA can cause ventricular fibrillation.
Answer0.5 mA unnoticed; 15 mA cannot let go; 100 mA may be fatal
Question 43
A 30 mA residual current device protects a socket. State what it does and how fast it must act.
Solution
1It compares the current in the live and neutral conductors.
2A difference of 30 mA or more means current is leaking, perhaps through a person, and the device opens.
3It must act within 300 ms at 30 mA and within 40 ms at 150 mA.
Answertrips on 30 mA leakage within 300 ms
Question 44
An earth fault loop impedance is 0.5 Ω on 230 V. A 16 A type B breaker trips at 5 times its rating. Find the fault current and say whether the breaker trips quickly.
Solution
1Fault current = 230/0.5 = 460 A.
2Trip current = 5 × 16 = 80 A.
3460 A is well above 80 A, so the breaker opens almost at once.
Answer460 A; trips quickly
Question 45
A heater is rated 2.3 kW at 230 V. Find the current and the fuse to choose from 3, 5, 13 A.
Solution
1I = P/V = 2300/230 = 10 A.
2The fuse must be above the load current but protect the cable: 13 A.
Answer10 A; 13 A fuse
Question 46
A cable carries 10 A and has a resistance of 0.2 Ω. Find the heat produced in it.
Solution
1P = I2R = 102 × 0.2 = 20 W.
2Overloaded cables heat up and the insulation fails first.
Answer20 W
Question 47
List the steps of lockout and tagout.
Solution
1Plan and tell the people affected.
2Shut down the equipment.
3Isolate every energy source.
4Fit your own lock and tag to each isolator.
5Release or block stored energy.
6Prove it is dead by attempting a start.
7Only the person who fitted the lock removes it.
Answernotify, shut down, isolate, lock and tag, release stored energy, verify
Question 48
Two 2 kW heaters are plugged into one 13 A extension lead on 230 V. Is it safe?
Solution
1Total power = 4000 W.
2Current = 4000/230 = 17.4 A.
3That exceeds 13 A, so the lead overheats. Use separate sockets.
Answerno; 17.4 A exceeds 13 A
Common slips: Add the power of every item on a socket before you plug them in.
Question 49
Why do construction sites use 110 V tools supplied through a centre-tapped transformer?
Solution
1The centre point is earthed, so each conductor is at 55 V to earth.
2The most a person can touch against earth is 55 V rather than 230 V, so a shock is far less likely to be dangerous.
Answerno conductor is more than 55 V from earth
Question 50
A tester shows a circuit is dead. Is the work safe to begin?
Solution
1The tester itself may be faulty.
2Prove the tester on a known live source, test the circuit, then prove the tester again.
3This is called prove-test-prove.
Answeronly after prove-test-prove
CHAPTER 2 · BLOCK 6

Mechanical hazards

Questions 51–60. Ladders, slings, falls, guards and lifting.

Question 51
A ladder must reach 6 m up a wall. Using the 1-in-4 rule, find how far the foot stands from the wall.
Solution
1The foot is 1 m out for every 4 m of height.
2Distance = 6/4 = 1.5 m.
3Tie the top, and extend the ladder 1 m above the landing.
Answer1.5 m
Question 52
A 1000 kg load hangs from a two-leg sling. Each leg makes 60° with the horizontal. Find the tension in each leg.
Solution
1Weight = 1000 × 9.81 = 9810 N.
2The vertical parts of the two tensions carry it: 2T sin 60° = 9810.
3T = 9810/(2 × 0.866) = 5664 N.
Answer5664 N per leg
Question 53
The same load hangs with each leg at 30° to the horizontal. Find the tension in each leg.
Solution
12T sin 30° = 9810.
2T = 9810/(2 × 0.5) = 9810 N.
3The tension is 1.73 times greater than at 60°.
Answer9810 N per leg
Common slips: Shallow sling angles multiply the load in each leg. Keep the angle at 45° or more from the horizontal.
Question 54
A sling has a safe working load of 2 t and a safety factor of 5. Find its breaking load.
Solution
1Breaking load = SWL × factor of safety.
2= 2 × 5 = 10 t.
Answer10 t
Question 55
A worker on a 1 m lanyard may fall 2 m before the line tightens. Find the fall factor.
Solution
1Fall factor = fall distance/lanyard length.
2= 2/1 = 2.
3This is the most severe case; the forces on the body are large, so a shock absorber is needed.
Answer2.0
Question 56
An 80 kg worker falls freely through 2 m. Find the speed and energy at the end of the fall.
Solution
1v = √(2gh) = √(2 × 9.81 × 2) = 6.26 m/s.
2E = mgh = 80 × 9.81 × 2 = 1570 J.
3The harness and absorber must take this energy over a safe distance.
Answer6.26 m/s; 1570 J
Question 57
A press stops 0.3 s after the stop signal. A hand moves at 1.6 m/s. Find the least distance between the guard and the danger point.
Solution
1Distance = speed × stopping time.
2= 1.6 × 0.3 = 0.48 m.
3Add the allowance in the standard for reach.
Answer0.48 m, plus the allowance
Question 58
A 40-bag delivery of 50 kg cement is moved by a trolley rated for 200 kg. Find the number of trips and the load per trip in bags.
Solution
1Total mass = 40 × 50 = 2000 kg.
2Trips = 2000/200 = 10.
3Each trip carries 200/50 = 4 bags.
Answer10 trips of 4 bags
Common slips: A 50 kg bag exceeds common one-person lifting guidelines of about 25 kg, so use the trolley.
Question 59
A forklift is rated 2000 kg at a load centre of 500 mm. The load centre is 750 mm. Find the derated capacity.
Solution
1Capacity falls in proportion to the load centre distance.
22000 × 500/750 = 1333 kg.
Answer1333 kg
Question 60
A scaffold platform of 5 m2 is rated 2.0 kN/m2. Six workers of 80 kg and 300 kg of materials stand on it. Is it within its rating?
Solution
1Capacity = 2.0 × 5 = 10 kN.
2Load = (6 × 80 + 300) × 9.81 = 780 × 9.81 = 7.65 kN.
37.65 kN < 10 kN, so it is within its rating, with a margin of 2.35 kN.
Answeryes; 7.65 kN against 10 kN
CHAPTER 2 · BLOCK 7

Chemical and environmental hazards

Questions 61–70. Exposure, ventilation, confined spaces and storage.

Question 61
Convert 50 ppm of carbon monoxide (molar mass 28.01) to mg/m3 at 25 °C and 1 atm.
Solution
1mg/m3 = ppm × molar mass/24.45.
2= 50 × 28.01/24.45 = 57.3 mg/m3.
Answer57.3 mg/m3
Question 62
A worker is exposed to 80 ppm of a solvent for 2 h and 20 ppm for 6 h. Find the 8-hour time-weighted average.
Solution
1TWA = Σ(C × t)/8.
2= (80 × 2 + 20 × 6)/8 = 280/8 = 35 ppm.
Answer35 ppm
Question 63
A process releases 20 mg of vapour a minute. The limit is 10 mg/m3. Find the dilution airflow for perfect mixing and with a mixing factor of 3.
Solution
1Perfect mixing: Q = 20/10 = 2 m3/min.
2Real rooms mix poorly, so multiply by 3: 6 m3/min.
Answer2 m3/min; 6 m3/min with the factor
Common slips: Local exhaust at the source is better than dilution for toxic vapours.
Question 64
A room of 6 m × 5 m × 3 m has 270 m3/h of fresh air. Find the air changes per hour.
Solution
1Volume = 6 × 5 × 3 = 90 m3.
2ACH = 270/90 = 3.
Answer3 air changes per hour
Question 65
A confined-space test shows 17% oxygen. May you enter?
Solution
1Safe air holds between 19.5% and 23.5% oxygen.
217% is below the range, so do not enter.
3Ventilate, retest, and test oxygen, then flammable gas, then toxic gas, before entry.
Answerno; below 19.5%
Question 66
Match the pictogram to the hazard: flame, skull and crossbones, corrosion, exclamation mark.
Solution
1Flame: flammable.
2Skull and crossbones: acute toxicity, which can kill.
3Corrosion: burns skin and eyes, and attacks metals.
4Exclamation mark: irritant, or harmful at lower levels.
Answerflammable; acute toxic; corrosive; irritant
Question 67
Which pairs must not be stored together: (a) acid and bleach; (b) oxidiser and fuel; (c) two boxes of paper?
Solution
1(a) acid and bleach release chlorine gas.
2(b) an oxidiser feeds a fire in the fuel.
3(c) paper with paper is not an incompatibility.
Answer(a) and (b)
Question 68
A worker breathes 30 ppm for 4 h. Find the exposure in ppm·hours, and compare with 8 h at 15 ppm.
Solution
1Exposure = 30 × 4 = 120 ppm·h.
28 h at 15 ppm = 120 ppm·h, the same total.
3Some substances act on the total dose; others on the peak, so check the limit type.
Answer120 ppm·h; equal
Question 69
Which sections of a safety data sheet give first aid and personal protection?
Solution
1Section 4 is first-aid measures.
2Section 8 covers exposure controls and personal protection.
3Sections 5 and 7 cover fire-fighting and handling and storage.
Answersections 4 and 8
Question 70
Prepare 1 L of 1 M acid from 18 M stock. Find the volume of stock and the order of mixing.
Solution
1C1V1 = C2V2.
2V1 = 1 × 1000/18 = 55.6 mL.
3Pour the acid slowly into about 900 mL of water, never the reverse, then make up to 1 L.
Answer55.6 mL, acid added to water
Common slips: Water added to concentrated acid can boil and spit.

Questions 71–80. Illuminance, heat stress, posture and vibration.

2= 800/4 = 200 lx.
